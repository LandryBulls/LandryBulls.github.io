The Challenge of Participation

Whether we're engaged in casual dinner conversations, collaborating on team projects, or dancing with friends, our daily social interactions depend on our ability to maintain a shared understanding with multiple people at once. This shared understanding is dynamic and continuously evolving, shaped through subtle negotiations during each exchange. Live social interactions requires that we simultaneously track multiple dimensions of information - the flow of conversation, the emotional states of our interaction partners, and the group's collective progress toward common goals. This requires the integration of a complex array of social signals that arrive through multiple sensory channels, originate from different individuals, and derive their meaning from the relationships between participants rather than existing in isolation.

Visually, social information is embedded in both facial expressions and body language. In coordination with these visual cues, the acoustic properties of speech also help guide our understanding of others' conversational expression. In addition to these social cues being multimodal, they are also irreducibly relative; decoding them depends on an integration of cues coming from different people, as is the case when determining which individuals in a crowd are speaking to one another or when interpreting one person's facial expressions in the context of what another is saying.

Despite this complexity, humans have a remarkable capacity to navigate these dynamic social systems with ease. My research investigates the recurring patterns that emerge in face-to-face social interactions. I explore how our brains may be naturally equipped to identify and interpret these patterns as building blocks of social life. Effectively perceiving and partipating in these patterns underpins our ability to engage appropriately in social situations and accurately perceive others' mental states, personality traits, relationships, and roles within their social groups. In other words, I study participation: the psychological and neural mechanisms that allow us to take a birds-eye view of the social world and use this perspective to guide contextually informed behavior.

The core of my research can be broken down into a few subquestions:

  • How do people coordinate group conversations using multimodal signaling?
  • How does the brain segment social events during conversations, and how might people embody this segmentation process through nonverbal communication?
  • How might distributed patterns in multimodal communication between two or more individuals index the nature of their interpersonal relationships?

Current Projects

Identifying collective social states in free-flowing group conversations

Group conversations naturally flow between different collective states - from moments of shared laughter, to passionate debates, to intimate gossip. I'm fascinated by how people coordinate their communication to navigate between these states. What psychological dimensions define these different states? And what are the downstream consequences of these states on peoples relationships, sense of self, and well-being?

To explore these questions, I've built a rich dataset of natural group conversations, captured through multiple camera angles and individual microphones worn by participants. From these recordings, I use pre-trained deep neural networks to extract various expressive modalities, including facial expressions, body movements in 3D space, voice patterns, and speech content. I complement this multimodal signal data with surveys completed by participants before and after each conversation designed to capture their subjective experience of the conversation, personality traits, and attitudes towards one another.

My research focuses on identifying recurring patterns in these multimodal social signals that might structure people's collective navigation through the conceptual space of group conversations. By feeding the social signals detected by pre-trained DNNs into new neural networks, I aim to understand the natural statistical structure of group conversations and how variations on this structure might index different psychological phenomena, from the the formation of social roles to learning about the self through others.

While the more complex analyses are ongoing, initial findings have already revealed interesting patterns. For instance, participants consistently agree in their ratings of each other's extraversion, which strongly correlates with how much someone speaks during the conversation. This supports psychological theories that view extraversion as fundamentally linked to verbal engagement. Additionally, we've found that people feel more connected to those who ask more questions during group conversations, independent of how much they speak overall.

In future work, I plan to integrate a neuroimaging component to this paradigm to better understand whether the same statistical regularities that artificial neural networks are able to identify have a priveleged representation in the brain.

As my main project, these analyses are in active development, and I look forward to sharing their results here!
