Group conversations naturally flow between different collective states - from moments of shared laughter, to passionate debates, to intimate gossip. I'm fascinated by how people orchestrate interpersonal signals to collaboratively navigate between these states. What psychological dimensions define these different states? And what are the downstream consequences of these states on peoples relationships, sense of self, and well-being?

To explore these questions, I've built a rich dataset of natural group conversations, captured through multiple camera angles and individual microphones worn by participants. From these recordings, I use pre-trained deep neural networks to extract various expressive modalities, including facial expressions, body movements in 3D space, voice patterns, and speech content. I compliment this multimodal time series data with surveys completed by participants before and after each conversation designed to capture their subjective experience of the conversation, personality traits, and attitudes towards one another.

I'm working on identifying recurring patterns in these signals that might index how people shape each other's minds. By modeling these data using a complex-systems and information-theoretic framework, I aim to understand the natural statistical structure of group conversations and how variations on this structure might index different psychological phenomena, from the formation of social roles to learning about the self through others.

While the more complex analyses are ongoing, initial findings have already revealed interesting patterns. For instance, participants consistently agree in their ratings of each other's extraversion, which strongly correlates with how much someone speaks during the conversation. This supports psychological theories that view extraversion as fundamentally linked to verbal engagement. We've also found that people feel more connected to those who ask more questions during group conversations, independent of how much they speak overall. Also evident in this data is that people are using conversational behavior to infer each other's personalities. That is, the more simililar any two people's manneristic "style", the more similar others in the group find their personality to be.

This paradigm offers rich insight into personality research. The presence of multiple raters for each person, where each participant's personality is estimated by multiple people at the same time, combined with the precise measurement of conversational behavior, allows us to better understand how our interactions shape how we see each other. In order to arrive at a more ecologically valid representation of personality, we may need to focus more on individuals' behavior when it is specifically conditioned on the overt behaviors of others during interactions.
