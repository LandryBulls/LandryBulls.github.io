Human social interaction is deeply embodied. The precise control we have over our face, body, and vocal chords combined with fine sensory acuity allows us to establish rich information streams with each other at a moment's notice. Through intricately sequenced facial expressions, gesture, and speech, we immerse each other in our inner worlds through storytelling, share our joy and grief during heartfelt dialogue, and develop complex plans to address future challenges. Despite recent innovations in social media, our brains evolved first and foremost to understand each other through face-to-face interactions.

My research focuses on <b>participation</b>: the psychological and neural mechanisms that allow us to take a birds-eye view of embodied social situations and use this perspective to guide contextually informed behavior. I am specifically interested in how we manage a seemingly dual role in contexts like group conversations, where we often orchestrate our expressions toward multiple people while simultaneously being one of many recipients of another's expressions. In these settings, the locus of causality becomes distributed rather than concentrated. 

Despite all this, people navigate most social situations with seasoned intuition. The foundation of this intuition is that while not all conversations are the same, they are made of the same ingredients—emergent, relational patterns like mutual nods of agreement, sequences of vocal intonation that distinguish playful riffing from serious debate, or subtle patterns of eye contact that signal close friendship. These embodied orchestrations are the visceral core of shared reality. 

Importantly, these phenomena exist as relations between people; they are irreducibly relative and may or may not involve oneself. Scientifically, we are only at the very beginning of understanding of how distributed social signals index our constantly changing relationships and their sensory grounding. What can the emergent dynamics of groups tell us about the minds of their constitients?
